# Office Hours: RepayRoute

Generated 2026-09-29 · Startup mode · Pre-product · Decision record

## Position

“A student-loan content site for 7 million people” is too broad and too easy to commoditize. The credible wedge is **a payment-change notice companion**: help one borrower turn a confusing letter into a quantified comparison and a precise call-preparation checklist in under five minutes. The prototype tests that wedge; it does not claim demand has been proven.

## Six forcing questions

### 1. Demand reality

The source offers market signals—millions potentially affected and high search volume—but no evidence that a named borrower has paid. Searches prove anxiety, not willingness to pay. Demand is unproven until borrowers complete the workflow, return with a second notice, or pay for human-reviewed help.

### 2. Painful status quo

An affected borrower reads a servicer notice, searches conflicting articles, opens StudentAid.gov’s simulator, tries to infer which facts apply, calls a servicer, and often fails to preserve the notice or confirmation number. The cost is not only time: a misunderstood deadline or unaffordable autopay change can create cash-flow shock. The real competitor is this browser-tabs-plus-phone-notes workaround.

### 3. Desperate specificity

The first customer is **Maya, a public-school employee with federal loans, four years of possible PSLF history, and a payment-change notice she cannot translate into a budget decision**. She fears losing qualifying-payment progress or accepting a payment she cannot carry. “All student borrowers” is rejected as an ICP.

### 4. Narrowest paid wedge

The smallest paid product is a $29 human-reviewed “notice-to-call brief”: extract the notice, compare the user-entered payment with a transparent standard amortization, identify documents to gather, and generate questions for the servicer. The free self-serve brief shown here is the acquisition and learning loop. No plan recommendation or enrollment is in the wedge.

### 5. Observation and surprise

No unassisted borrower observation has occurred. Surveys and page views would be weak substitutes. Watch five affected borrowers use the prototype without coaching; record where they hesitate, whether they trust the estimate, which document they cannot find, and what they do next. A useful surprise would be users valuing the evidence checklist more than the calculator—evidence that the product is an accountability record, not a recommendation engine.

### 6. Future-fit

Policy churn makes generic explanatory content obsolete faster. RepayRoute becomes more valuable only if it maintains a dated, cited rule layer and a durable borrower evidence trail. If official tools become clearer and servicers become reliable, the calculator becomes less essential; the portable case record and human escalation network remain defensible.

## Premise challenge

1. The problem is not “choosing the best plan”; it is reducing uncertainty before a consequential call.
2. A deterministic, inspectable calculation earns more trust than simulated AI for the first wedge.
3. The first release must avoid regulated-advice posture and collect no sensitive borrower data.

## Approaches considered

### A — Content and quiz funnel (minimal viable)

Effort S · Risk medium. Publish explainers and a quiz that emails a checklist. Fast demand test, but weak product value and an undifferentiated moat.

### B — Notice-to-call brief (selected)

Effort M · Risk medium. Transparent calculations, pressure flag, PSLF verification prompt, and a next-step checklist. Delivers value without claiming to choose a plan and creates observable workflow behavior.

### C — Connected repayment case manager (ideal architecture)

Effort XL · Risk high. Import servicer records, maintain policy rules, collaborate with counselors, and track disputes. Stronger retention and data moat, but security, regulatory, and integration scope are inappropriate before demand proof.

## Success criteria

- 5/5 observed borrowers finish without founder help.
- At least 3/5 use the checklist during a real servicer interaction.
- At least 2/10 qualified users pay $29 for a human-reviewed brief.
- Zero users confuse an illustration with an official quote in comprehension interviews.

## Assignment

Recruit five borrowers who received a recent payment-change notice and silently observe each using the brief. Do not demo it. Ask them to explain, in their own words, what they believe the result means and what they will do next.
